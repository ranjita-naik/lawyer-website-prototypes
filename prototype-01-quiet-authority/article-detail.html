When Should a Business Consider Arbitration Over Litigation?

Arjun Mehta, Advocate

General informational note

Headnote

What the choice between arbitration and the courts actually turns on — subject knowledge, privacy, control of the timetable, the reach of the tribunal — and why it is a decision made at the drafting table rather than at the point of dispute.

The question is usually asked too late. By the time a dispute has arisen, the choice between arbitration and the courts has generally already been made — in a clause drafted months or years earlier, often at the end of a negotiation when everyone had tired of the document. Whether that clause was the right one is worth thinking about before it is needed rather than after.

Arbitration tends to suit disputes where the parties value three things: a decision-maker with subject knowledge, a degree of privacy, and a process the parties can shape. Construction, technology, distribution and commercial lease disputes often fall into this group, because the facts are technical and the parties may well continue dealing with one another afterwards. Arbitration also travels: awards are, as a rule, more readily enforced across borders than court decrees.

Litigation has advantages that are easy to undervalue. Court proceedings are public, which occasionally matters. Interim relief from a court can be robust and quickly obtained. Appeals are structured and familiar. And where a dispute involves parties who never signed the contract — a guarantor, a group company, a subsequent purchaser — a court can usually deal with all of them together, while a tribunal is confined to those bound by the arbitration agreement. Some disputes are not suited to arbitration at all: certain categories of claim are treated as non-arbitrable, and insolvency proceedings follow their own statutory course.

Cost is the least reliable basis for the decision. Arbitration is often described as the cheaper route; in practice the parties themselves bear tribunal fees, institutional charges and venue costs, and a poorly managed arbitration can cost considerably more than a well-managed suit. What arbitration offers more dependably is control — over who decides, over the timetable, and over how much process the parties wish to build.

The practical suggestion is a modest one. Read the dispute-resolution clause in your standard contracts as a commercial term rather than a formality, and decide deliberately where you would prefer to be if the relationship goes wrong. That decision is far cheaper to make on a quiet afternoon than in the week a notice arrives.
