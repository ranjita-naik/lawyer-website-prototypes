Constitutional and Administrative Matters

In brief

Representing clients in matters involving government authorities, regulatory decisions and constitutional remedies before the High Court. Much of the work lies in reading the record closely, and in understanding how a decision was arrived at before it is questioned.

Most disputes with the State begin as something smaller: a licence refused, a tender cancelled, an approval withheld, an order passed without a hearing, a demand raised on a reading of a rule the recipient did not expect. The remedy in these matters is generally the writ jurisdiction of the High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution, and the practice in this area is built around it.

Judicial review is a narrower instrument than it is often assumed to be. A court exercising writ jurisdiction examines how a decision was reached rather than substituting its own view of what the decision should have been. Whether the authority had the power it exercised, whether it considered what it was required to consider, whether the person affected was heard, whether reasons were given, and whether those reasons hold together — these are the questions that decide such matters. A petition that argues the merits and neglects the process is usually the weaker petition.

Three practical questions therefore come first, before any drafting. Is there an alternative statutory remedy — an appeal or a revision — that ought to be exhausted, or good reason why it need not be? How much time has passed, and can the delay be explained? And what relief is actually available: is the client seeking to set an order aside, to have the matter decided afresh by the authority, or simply to compel a decision that has not been taken at all? The answers frequently reshape the case, and occasionally suggest that a representation to the authority is the better first step.

The work itself is unglamorous and largely documentary. Files are obtained, the sequence of official action is reconstructed, notings and correspondence are read in order, and the statutory framework is traced from the enabling provision downward. A matter properly assembled at that stage tends to argue itself.

Matters typically include

  • Challenges to orders of statutory and regulatory authorities
  • Licensing, permission and approval refusals
  • Tender and public procurement decisions
  • Failure or refusal of an authority to decide
  • Enforcement of statutory duties and reasoned-order requirements
