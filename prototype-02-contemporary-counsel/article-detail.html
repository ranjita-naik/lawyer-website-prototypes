Understanding Arbitration Clauses in Commercial Contracts

The provision negotiated last is often the first one to matter.

An arbitration clause is often one of the last provisions negotiated in a commercial contract, and one of the first to matter when the relationship goes wrong. It usually receives less attention than the commercial terms around it, and that imbalance tends to show up at exactly the wrong moment.

At its simplest, the clause records that the parties will resolve disputes by arbitration rather than through the courts. In India, such agreements are governed by the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. For the clause to operate as intended, the agreement to arbitrate needs to be in writing and needs to be clear about what it covers.

Beyond that, a handful of drafting choices tend to determine how smoothly a dispute actually proceeds.

Scope. Does the clause reach all disputes “arising out of or in connection with” the contract, or only a narrower category? Narrow wording invites a preliminary argument about whether a particular claim belongs in arbitration at all — time and cost spent before the dispute itself is reached.

Seat and venue. The seat determines which courts exercise supervisory jurisdiction over the arbitration. The venue is simply where hearings are convened. Contracts frequently use the two words interchangeably, and that ambiguity has been litigated more often than one might expect.

Number of arbitrators and method of appointment. A sole arbitrator is generally faster and less expensive; a three-member tribunal may suit higher-value or more technical matters. Either way, the clause should set out how the appointment is to be made if the parties cannot agree between themselves.

Institutional or ad-hoc. Institutional arbitration brings a published set of rules, a fee schedule and administrative support. Ad-hoc arbitration offers flexibility, but places more of the procedural burden on the parties and their counsel.

Language and governing law. Both are straightforward to state at the drafting stage and awkward to resolve afterwards.

A clause that addresses these points in a few clear sentences will usually serve the parties better than a longer one assembled from unrelated precedents. Where a contract matters to the business, its arbitration clause is worth reading with the same care as the commercial terms.
