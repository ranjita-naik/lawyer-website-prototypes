A Practical Note on Mediation as a First Step

What actually happens in a mediation, and when it is worth trying.

Written by Arjun Mehta, Advocate · General informational note

Mediation is usually introduced as an alternative to going to court, which makes it sound like the smaller option — something to fall back on when litigation is too slow or too expensive. That framing is misleading. Mediation is a different process with a different purpose, and in several kinds of dispute it is the first thing worth considering rather than the last.

What it actually involves

In a mediation the parties sit with a trained neutral third person whose job is not to decide anything. The mediator has no power to impose an outcome. What the mediator does is manage the conversation: separating the issues that are genuinely in dispute from those that are not, meeting the parties together and separately, and testing whether a settlement both sides can live with exists at all. Advocates commonly attend, and their role shifts from arguing a case to advising on whether a proposed arrangement is sound.

The process is confidential, and what is said in the course of it generally cannot be used later in court. Nothing binds anyone until an agreement is signed. If no agreement is reached, the parties are where they started, with the option of proceeding.

Where it sits in Indian practice

Mediation is no longer informal. Courts have long had the power under Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure to refer disputes for settlement, including to mediation. For most commercial disputes that do not involve urgent interim relief, pre-institution mediation is a statutory requirement before a suit can be filed. The Mediation Act, 2023 has since given the process a broader statutory framework, and provides for a mediated settlement agreement to be enforceable in the same manner as a judgment or decree of a court.

When it is worth considering first

The strongest case for mediation is a dispute between parties who will still have a relationship afterwards — siblings dividing an inheritance, co-owners of a family property, a landlord and a long-standing tenant, or business partners who intend to continue trading. A court can decide who is entitled to what. It cannot decide how a family will speak to one another for the next twenty years.

It is also worth considering where the real obstacle is not the law but the accumulation of grievances around it. A mediation can put those on the table in a way that pleadings cannot.

Mediation is not always appropriate. Where a genuine question of law needs an authoritative answer, where urgent interim protection is required, or where one party has no real intention of settling, it can add delay without adding value. The honest answer in a first conversation is sometimes that mediation would not help in your matter.

In short

  • A mediator manages the conversation but decides nothing.
  • The process is confidential and nothing binds until it is signed.
  • A mediated settlement agreement is enforceable like a decree.
  • It suits parties whose relationship will outlast the dispute.
  • It is not right for every matter, and should not be pretended otherwise.
